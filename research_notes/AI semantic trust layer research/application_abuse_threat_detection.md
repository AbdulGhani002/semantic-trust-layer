## 1. What are the strongest methods from 2018 to 2026 for toxicity, hate, threat and harassment detection, including transformers and LLMs?

### Takeaway
On curated tests, LLMs with a clear definition in the prompt match or beat fine-tuned transformers, and reasoning or argument structures add a few points on top. No method survives contact with representative data: across many models, average precision fell to 9.4% on a true random sample of tweets, against 40% on academic datasets.

### Inferences
- A sound STL design is staged: a fast transformer screen, an LLM reasoning step for uncertain or high-stakes messages, and human review for anything acted on. This follows Kumar, DuPL's early exit, and the HateDay and NaijaHate results in question 7.
- The agent prompt should carry a written definition and the institution's own policy, because Kumar and Patel both show definitions and in-context policy change results.
- Treat "threat" as its own output, not a sub-label of toxicity. Later sections show it behaves differently.
- Character-level input or text normalisation is worth having, because Roman Urdu spelling varies and attackers use the same variation (Gröndahl, Kurita).

## 2. How well do models detect implicit hate and veiled threats that contain no slur or keyword?

### Takeaway
Poorly with older tools and moderately with LLMs. Perspective's threat score caught almost no implicit threats (recall 0.005) while the best zero-shot LLM reached macro-F1 0.72, and the best run with semantic role labels reached 0.77. In one audit, three commercial APIs missed 52-64% of ToxiGen's hateful items, and Google missed fewer only by raising false alarms. There is one proper veiled-threat benchmark, it is English, and it is partly synthetic.

### Cited Findings

**Veiled and implicit threats**
- **Bruni, Bardazzi, Tesconi (2026). "ThreatCore: A Benchmark for Explicit and Implicit Threat Detection."** DATA 2026, Vol. 2, pp. 1127-1135. `[PR][FT]` [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.10563), [DOI](https://doi.org/10.5220/0015075600004091), [Repo](https://github.com/DavideBruni/ThreatCore). Method: strict threat definition, re-annotation of existing sets, three classes. Data: 15,691 re-annotated plus about 6,000 synthetic, total 21,764. Results by model:
  - Perspective threat score: explicit precision 0.962, recall 0.221; implicit recall 0.005; macro-F1 0.333.
  - ModernBERT zero-shot: implicit recall 0.901, but non-threat recall only 0.092; macro-F1 0.502.
  - Zero-shot LLMs, macro-F1: GPT-oss:20b 0.721, GPT-4o-mini 0.683, Grok-3 0.673, Phi4:14b 0.663, Qwen2.5:7b 0.612, Llama3.3:70b 0.565. Llama3.3:70b recalled only 0.322 of non-threats, so it over-flags.
  - With semantic role labels in the prompt: Phi4:14b rose to macro-F1 0.773, implicit-threat F1 0.749.
  - Limit: English only, and a large synthetic share.
- ThreatCore also shows threat labels in standard data are loose. Of 666 threat-labelled Latent Hatred items, 453 became non-threat under its definition. Of 918 in Gab, 494 did. Of 1,457 in Jigsaw Unintended Bias, 325 did and only 56 were implicit. In one source only 21 of 4,088 candidates were real threats — [ThreatCore](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.10563).
- **Raza, Meghji, Mahoto, Al Reshan, Abosaq, Sulaiman, Shaikh (2024). "Reading Between the Lines: Machine Learning Ensemble and Deep Learning for Implied Threat Detection in Textual Data."** International Journal of Computational Intelligence Systems. `[PR][AB]` [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s44196-024-00580-y). Method: classical, ensemble and deep models on lexical and linguistic features. Data: a purpose-built machine-generated dataset. Result: BiLSTM F1 91.61%, DNN 91.49%, logistic regression 77.13%. Limit*: synthetic data, so the high scores should not be expected on real messages.
- **Röttger, Vidgen, Nguyen, Waseem, Margetts, Pierrehumbert (2021). "HateCheck: Functional Tests for Hate Speech Detection Models."** ACL 2021. `[PR][FT]` [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.15606), [DOI](https://doi.org/10.18653/v1/2021.acl-long.4). On direct threats and normative threats against a group, two BERT models scored 87.2/91.4% and 75.9/83.6%, Perspective 100/100%, SiftNinja 4.5/2.1%. Limit*: these test cases are explicit and name the group, so high scores say nothing about veiled threats.

**Implicit hate benchmarks**
- **ElSherief, Ziems, Muchlinski, Anupindi, Seybolt, De Choudhury, Yang (2021). ### Inferences
- The STL brief's requirement, threats with no bad words, is the hardest published case. A realistic target is macro-F1 near 0.7-0.8 on a clean balanced test, and much lower precision at inbox base rates.
- ThreatCore's two findings fit together for STL: LLMs work best, and adding structure (who does what to whom) helps. An agent step that extracts actor, action and target, then reasons about intent, is directly supported.
- Over-flagging is a bigger risk for implicit threats than for explicit ones. Several LLMs reached high implicit recall only by flagging many benign items.
- Metrics should be reported per class. A macro score alone hides Perspective's near-zero implicit recall.

### Gaps
- No public benchmark found for veiled threats in email, formal letters or Urdu.
- No evaluation found of models on threats aimed at a named individual in an institution, such as a Vice Chancellor or Rector.
- A [Dutch master's thesis on legally actionable threats](https://studenttheses.uu.nl/bitstream/handle/20.500.12932/43817/MBI_Thesis_NoudJan_de_Rijk_Classifying_Legally_Actionable_Threats_using_Language_Models.pdf?sequence=1) and [Spitzberg and Gawron (2016)](https://commons.erau.edu/jdfsl/vol11/iss3/7/) appeared in search. I did not open either, and the second is before the date range.
- Intent to act, as opposed to threatening wording, has no labelled benchmark I could find.

## 3. What is known about false positives, such as bias against identity terms and quoted or reclaimed slurs?

### Takeaway
False positives are systematic. Models treat identity words, dialects and any mention of a slur as evidence of abuse, so they punish targets of abuse, people who quote abuse, and people who reclaim a word. A chain-of-thought prompt that teaches use versus mention cut false positives on counterspeech by 82.61% with only a 2.99% drop in true positives.

### Cited Findings

**Quoted slurs, counterspeech, use versus mention**
- **Gligoric, Cheng, Zheng, Durmus, Jurafsky (2024). "NLP Systems That Can't Tell Use from Mention Censor Counterspeech, but Teaching the Distinction Helps."** NAACL 2024. `[PR][FT]` [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.01651). Result: a ToxiGen-trained model and Perspective had over 20% false positive rate on counterspeech, gpt-3.5-turbo 11.11%, gpt-4 8.89%. Chain-of-thought mitigation cut this by 82.61% on hate speech, while true positives fell 2.99%. Limit: online text often lacks quote marks that signal mention.
- **HateCheck** ([Röttger et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.15606)) `[PR][FT]`. Accuracy on non-hateful test cases: reclaimed slurs 39.5% and 33.3% for two BERT models, 28.4% Perspective, 18.5% SiftNinja. Denouncing hate by quoting it: 26.6%, 32.9%, 15.6%, 79.8%. Negated hate: 12.8%, 12.0%, 3.8% for the first three. Neutral mention of a group: 20.6%, 58.7%, 84.1%, 100%.
- **Yu, Blanco, Hong (2022). "Hate Speech and Counter Speech Detection: Conversational Context Does Matter."** NAACL 2022. `[PR][AB]` [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.06423). Result: human labels change for most comments when the prior comment is shown, and neural models do better with context.
- **Pavlopoulos, Sorensen, Dixon, Thain, Androutsopoulos (2020). "Toxicity Detection: Does Context Really Matter?"** arXiv record lists no venue, commonly cited as ACL 2020, not confirmed here. `[PP][AB]` [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.00998). - **Menini, Aprosio, Tonelli (2021). "Abuse is Contextual, What about NLP? The Role of Context in Abusive Language Annotation and Detection."** arXiv. `[PP][AB]` [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.14916). Result: context-aware classification is harder but closer to real use.

- **Goyal, Kivlichan, Rosen, Vasserman (2022). "Is Your Toxicity My Toxicity? Exploring the Impact of Rater Identity on Toxicity Annotation."** CSCW 2022. `[PR][AB]` [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.00501). Result: rater identity significantly affects labels on identity-related comments.
- **Sap, Swayamdipta, Vianna, Zhou, Choi, Smith (2022). "Annotators with Attitudes: How Annotator Beliefs And Identities Bias Toxic Language Detection."** NAACL 2022. `[PR][AB]` [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2111.07997). Result: identity and beliefs shape labels.

Result: personal tuning improves accuracy by 86% on average.
- **Kumar et al. (2024)** ([ICWSM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.14517)) `[PR][FT]`: in a sample of errors, LLM false positives most often came from triggering on rough language and stereotypes (34%). Authors agreed with the model on 48% of false positives, so labels are noisy too.
- **Unitary's Detoxify card** states that swear words make a comment likely to be flagged toxic regardless of intent or tone, which can harm vulnerable groups — [model card](https://huggingface.co/unitary/toxic-bert).

### Inferences
- For the Rector example, a slur inside an email can mean three things: abuse of the reader, a report quoting someone else, or a discussion of the word. An STL agent needs an explicit use-or-mention step before it scores the message. The Gligoric mitigation is the strongest published evidence for doing this.
- Slur lists remain useful as a signal, but they should trigger review, not a verdict.
- South Asian identity terms and honorifics are likely false-positive triggers in tools trained on US data. This is inferred from Ghosh and Tonneau, not measured on Urdu.
- Track false positive rate separately on identity mentions, using threshold-free metrics in the style of Borkan.
- The label set should define what happens with quoted abuse, reclaimed use and complaints that repeat abuse. Annotators disagree on these, so decide policy before labelling.

### Gaps
- No Urdu or Roman Urdu test set found for false positives on identity terms, religious terms or quoted abuse. This looks like a real gap and a possible STL contribution.
- No measurement found of a false positive rate in a real institutional inbox.
- [QueerReclaimLex data](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.00020) and the use-mention sets are English. Whether the mitigation transfers to Urdu is unknown.

## 4. What work exists on email or workplace messages, as opposed to social media comments?

### Takeaway
Very little. One peer-reviewed enterprise email corpus exists, ToxiScope, and its text sits behind an LDC licence with fees. Its best model recalled only 43% of toxic sentences. The closest other evidence is software engineering chat and code review, where social media tools degrade badly, especially on formal writing. I found no public corpus of hate mail or threats sent to officials.

### Cited Findings
- **Bhat, Hosseini, Hassan Awadallah, Bennett, Li (2021). "Say 'YES' to Positivity: Detecting Toxic Language in Workplace Communications."** Findings of EMNLP 2021, pp. 2017-2029. `[PR-F][FT]` [Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2021.findings-emnlp.173/), [PDF](https://aclanthology.org/2021.findings-emnlp.173.pdf). PDF metadata may cut the author list, so check it on the Anthology page.
  - Method: taxonomy of offensive, impolite and negative gossip, with crowd labels and BERT plus context.
  - Data: ToxiScope, 10,110 sentences from the Avocado corporate email collection. The tables give 1,210 toxic (11.9%). The prose says 1,120, which looks like a typo.
  - Result: Perspective flagged only about 0.1% of Avocado, and the microaggression model had 0.12% precision. Best model BERT+MLP: toxic recall 0.43, non-toxic recall 0.9925, macro-F1 0.7696. Context lifted average category F1 from 0.464 to 0.521 for BERT with the previous email, and from 0.506 to 0.532 for BERT+MLP with the email body.
  - Limit: agreement was 0.77 on offensive but only 0.29 on impolite. Public offensive-language datasets are not suitable for workplace toxicity. Also, sampling was targeted, so 11.9% is not a true rate.
- **Access to ToxiScope:** the [repo](https://github.com/microsoft/ToxiScope) gives email id, text span and label only, with an MSR data licence file, and it has no SPDX licence. The text comes from the [Avocado collection at LDC](https://catalog.ldc.upenn.edu/LDC2015T03), 279 accounts of a defunct IT company, under individual or organisation agreements and fees.
- **Ferreira, Cheng, Adams (2021). "The 'Shut the f**k up' Phenomenon: Characterizing Incivility in Open Source Code Review Discussions."** ACM paper, recalled as CSCW 2021, venue string not confirmed. `[PR][AB]` [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3479497), [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.09905). Method: qualitative analysis. Data: 1,545 Linux Kernel Mailing List emails on rejected changes. Result: 66.66% of non-technical emails had uncivil features, mainly frustration, name calling and impatience. Limit*: no classifier, a public engineering list rather than a private inbox. Still the closest real-email study found.
- **Sarker, Turzo, Bosu (2020). "A Benchmark Study of the Contemporary Toxicity Detectors on Software Engineering Interactions."** APSEC 2020. `[PR][AB]` [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2009.09331). Data: 6,533 code review comments, 4,140 Gitter messages. Result: all tools degrade, and more on formal code review than on informal Gitter. Technical words such as "kill" and "dump" cause false alarms. Retraining helps. Limit*: English software text.
- **Sarker, Turzo, Dong, Bosu (2023). ### Inferences
- STL should plan on building its own small email test set. Reasonable sources are synthetic emails written by the team in Rector-office style, and public-email corpora with labels added by the team. Real university mail needs permission.
- Expect the opposite class mix to social media: formal tone, few obvious bad words, rare positives. Tools trained on tweets will under-detect, as ToxiScope and Sarker show.
- Adding email context is worthwhile. Unlike Wikipedia in Pavlopoulos, ToxiScope gained from it.
- Line-level labelling suits email. ToxiScope labels sentences, which also gives a natural way to highlight evidence for a human reviewer.

### Gaps
- I found no public corpus of threatening or hateful emails sent to public officials, universities or companies, and no Enron corpus labelled for abuse. Absence in my searches is not proof none exists.
- No paper found tests Urdu or Roman Urdu email.
- I could not read the [Kaggle "Email Classification (NLP)" page](https://www.kaggle.com/c/email-classificationnlp/overview/description). It rendered only its title. A search summary says it concerns abusive workplace email. Treat as unverified.
- No 2024-2026 LLM benchmark on Slack, Teams or email harassment was found. A [space-mission team study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.22679) came up, but it was not opened.

## 5. What exists for Urdu and Roman Urdu, and what datasets are public?

### Takeaway
There is real work: threat and abuse tasks at FIRE 2021, Roman Urdu hate corpora, a 72,771-comment parallel toxicity corpus, and first LLM studies in 2026. Threat detection is the weak spot: the only shared task topped out at F1 0.545, and a 2026 model fell from 90.42% to about 74.75% on that same data. Many headline scores are self-reported and unusually high, so treat them with care. I found nothing for implicit hate or veiled threats in Urdu.

### Inferences
- Report threat scores with the class mix. F1 0.545 at FIRE (about 18% threats in train) and 90.42% on a balanced set are not comparable, and the same TTLD model fell to about 74.75% on FIRE.
- A workable training base: Roman Urdu Toxic Corpus (CC-BY) plus RUHSOLD (MIT) for abuse, and the FIRE and Amjad sets for threats. Clear the Threatening_Dataset's licence with the authors first.
- Few-shot GPT-4 roughly equals fine-tuned BERT on RUHSOLD, and English prompts beat Urdu prompts. For STL agents, an English instruction with Urdu examples looks better than a fully Urdu prompt.
- LoRA fine-tuning is affordable for a three-person project and lifted F1 from 0.21-0.56 to about 0.91-0.94 within PURUTT. It still needs a test on another Roman Urdu corpus.
- Roman Urdu spelling variation points to normalisation, as in Bilal 2022, or to character-aware models, as in Lees 2022.

### Gaps
- No Urdu or Roman Urdu dataset found for implicit hate, veiled threats, false positives on identity terms, use versus mention, or email.
- No public Roman Urdu threat dataset found. The FIRE and Amjad data are in Urdu script.
- Could not confirm where FIRE 2021 data is hosted now. The [task site](https://www.urduthreat2021.cicling.org) gave no response to my check.
- Could not confirm a public PURUTT parallel release, HS-RU-20 availability, or RUTUBE and USADC locations.
- Code-mixed Urdu-English, common in Pakistani email, has only the MuRIL model above and no benchmark I could find.
- Perspective's Urdu support was not checked, because the API is closing anyway.

## 6. Which public datasets are standard (for example Jigsaw, Civil Comments, HateXplain, ToxiGen, Implicit Hate), under what licences, and what are their weaknesses?

### Takeaway
Licences are mostly open but messy: Hugging Face and GitHub often disagree, and tweet datasets carry platform terms the cards do not address. Shared weaknesses: social-media text, US and UK skew, keyword sampling, inflated positive rates, loose threat labels, and no email. HateCheck and ThreatCore are the most useful test assets, and HateDay is the most honest measure of real prevalence.

- Evaluation on biased academic datasets overestimates real performance by at least two-fold — [NaijaHate](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.19260). HateDay adds that academic data emphasises different target groups from those most hit in practice, and that hate versus offensive confusion is a main error — [HateDay](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.15462).
- Hate datasets strongly over-represent a few countries, US and UK for English — [Tonneau et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.17874).
- Real hate prevalence on Twitter was 0.7% on average and under 2% everywhere, 1.7% in India — [HateDay](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.15462). Dataset positive rates above 50% are therefore artificial, as in [DynHate's 54%](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.15761).
- Threat labels conflict with offensiveness and punitive rhetoric across sources — [ThreatCore](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.10563).

### Inferences
- Use CC0 and CC-BY sets for training, and keep ToxiGen, HateCheck and ThreatCore as separate stress tests, not as training data for the same evaluation.
- For a university project, record the source and licence of each dataset in a data sheet. Tweet-derived sets may carry platform rules beyond the listed licence. This is my caution, not a finding from the cards.
- Do not quote a single "accuracy" for STL. Report results on balanced stress tests and at a realistic low base rate.
- Re-annotating threat labels under one written definition, as ThreatCore did, is cheap and worthwhile before training a threat output.

### Gaps
- Kaggle competition rules for Jigsaw were not opened, so the CC0 tag rests on Hugging Face cards.
- Wikipedia text licensing in the Jigsaw data is not stated on the card, and I did not resolve it.
- Civil Comments' source platform terms beyond the card's CC0 statement were not checked.
- Licence status for THREAT, Founta, CAD and Gab Hate Corpus remains unverified.

## 7. Which pretrained models are public and how do they perform on independent tests?

### Takeaway
Public models post AUC scores of 0.92-0.99 on their own competitions and far lower elsewhere. On HateDay, the most honest test, no model reached both precision and recall above 50% in any context, and Perspective, not an open model, led on average. Perspective ends on 31 December 2026, so STL should plan around open models, or an LLM, plus its own evaluation.

### Cited Findings

**Service status**
- Google's Perspective API is being sunset. The site says the service stays active until 31 December 2026, with no new quota requests after February 2026 — [perspectiveapi.com](https://perspectiveapi.com/).

**Independent tests**
- **Tonneau, Liu, Malhotra, Hale, Fraiberger, Orozco-Olvera, Röttger (2025). "HateDay: Insights from a Global Hate Speech Dataset Representative of a Day on Twitter."** ACL 2025. `[PR][FT]` [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.15462). Data: 240,000 tweets, 93.4% full annotator agreement. Result: average precision 9.4% on HateDay, 40% on academic sets, 87.2% on HateCheck. Perspective averaged 44.3% across sets, beat open models in all languages but German, and led on the US at 12.3%. Llama 3.1 8B and Aya 23 8B were near 32.4%. Supervised beat zero-shot almost everywhere. Best F1 anywhere 47%, on India. Limit: authors call public models ill-suited to automatic moderation without substantial human oversight.
- **Tonneau, Quinta de Castro, Lasri, Farouq, Subramanian, Orozco-Olvera, Fraiberger (2024). "NaijaHate: Evaluating Hate Speech Detection on Nigerian Twitter Using Representative Data."** ACL 2024. `[PR][FT]` [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.19260). Result: 83-90% average precision on biased data against 34% on representative data. Reviewing the top 1% of flagged tweets moderates up to 60% of hate. Domain-adaptive pretraining and fine-tuning matter. Limit: review load is still about ten thousand tweets a day.
- **HateBench** ([Shen et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.16750)) `[PP][FT]`: F1 on LLM-generated hate: TweetHate 0.864, OpenAI Moderation 0.852, LFTW 0.825, Perspective 0.821, HSBERT 0.785, Detoxify original 0.782, Detoxify unbiased 0.730. Perspective fell from 0.878 on GPT-3.5 to 0.621 on GPT-4 text.
- **ThreatCore** ([Bruni et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.10563)) `[PR][FT]`: Perspective macro-F1 0.333, best zero-shot LLM 0.721, see question 2.
- **Watching the Watchers** ([Hartmann et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.14154)) `[PR-W][FT]`: on balanced Jigsaw, OpenAI ROC-AUC 78.6, Microsoft 75.8, Google 69.9. Google's false positive rate was 58.4%.
- **Kumar et al. 2024** ([ICWSM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.14517)) `[PR][FT]`: LLMs beat Perspective on Reddit toxicity, see question 1.
- **Latent Hatred** ([ElSherief et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.05322)) `[PR][FT]`: Perspective F 55.2 on implicit hate, BERT 68.9.
- **RealHarm** — Le Jeune, Liu, Rossi, Dora (2025), "RealHarm: A Collection of Real-World Language Model Application Failures", LLMSEC workshop `[PR-W][AB]` [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.10277). Moderation tools showed a real gap in protecting deployed AI agents. A search snippet says OpenAI Moderation and Perspective caught only 10-50% of unsafe conversations.
- **Safety-Flag** ([Hu 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.19072)) `[PP][FT]`: confidence ranking for human review helps some models far more than others, and calibration needs fixing before thresholds are trusted.
- **Multilingual HateCheck** — Röttger, Seelawi, Nozza, Talat, Vidgen (2022), WOAH 2022 `[PR-W][AB]` [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.09917). HateCheckHIn — Das, Saha, Mathew, Mukherjee (2022), LREC 2022 `[PR][AB]` [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.00328), [DOI](https://doi.org/10.63317/4wp8g25qy52j). Both show weak spots across languages, and HateCheckHIn adds code-mixed tests useful as a template for Urdu.

### Inferences
- No single public model is a safe default for STL. A reasonable open baseline is Cardiff's hate model or Detoxify for English, a LoRA-tuned model for Roman Urdu, and an LLM for threats. This is my judgement, not a tested pipeline.
- OpenAI's moderation endpoint is a useful free comparison, since it has explicit threatening categories. Sending private university email to an outside API raises data-protection issues the team must clear first. A local model suits a final year project better.
- Check any model's licence before shipping. Several popular ones show no licence field, which is not the same as permissive.
- Calibrate thresholds on STL's own data, as [Lost in Moderation](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.01623) and Safety-Flag recommend.
- Keep a human in the loop for any action on a flagged message. HateDay, NaijaHate and Lost in Moderation all point there.

### Gaps
- No independent audit found that measures Detoxify, Perspective and OpenAI's endpoint side by side on false positive rate for one dataset.
- No independent test found of any Urdu or Roman Urdu model on a second dataset, apart from TTLD on FIRE.
- HateBench's venue, ShieldGemma's and Granite Guardian's performance on hate and threat tasks, and current Perspective language coverage were not confirmed.
- In the ToxiGen row, the [Gligoric](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.01651) text says "Toxigen" without naming the exact checkpoint, so the link to tomh's models is my assumption.
- Hugging Face download counts change daily and do not reflect quality.
