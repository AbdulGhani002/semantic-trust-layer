# AI-based semantic detection of fraud and social engineering content (application layer)

Notes for the Semantic Trust Layer (STL) final year project. Scope: phishing email, business email compromise (BEC), SMS scams (smishing), malicious URLs, QR phishing (quishing), trojanised downloads. Sources checked up to 2026-11-05.

**How to read each entry**

- **Status.** `PR` = peer-reviewed; venue confirmed on Crossref, USENIX, CEUR, ACL Anthology or a DBLP conference or journal key. `PR?` = an arXiv comment or a citation index names a peer-reviewed venue, but I did not see the final proceedings record. `Pre` = preprint; no peer-reviewed version found. `Ind` = company post or press, not peer-reviewed.
- **Check.** `FT` = I read extracted full text. `Abs` = I read the abstract on a primary record (arXiv API, USENIX page, publisher metadata, OpenAlex). `Meta` = title, authors and venue confirmed only, so I report no method or result.
- **cit** = Semantic Scholar citation count on 2026-11-05, unless marked. Counts differ between indexes, and 2025 to 2026 papers look small only because they are new.
- **Limit** = stated by the authors, or plain from the study design. **Limit (mine)** = my own reading.
- Every listed paper was confirmed on a live record in this session. Anything weaker is marked inside the entry or listed under Gaps.
- Do not copy venues from Semantic Scholar without checking. It mapped IEEE CSR 2025 to "Computer Science Symposium in Russia" and IEEE ICMI 2024 to "International Conference on Multimodal Interaction". Venues below come from DOI records instead.

## What are the strongest and most cited methods from 2019 to 2026, and what results do they report?

### Takeaway

Four method families lead. Production systems that join text with behaviour or headers (BEC-Guard, lateral phishing, URLTran, CAPE) have the best evidence, because they report results at realistic false positive rates. Reference-based detectors (Phishpedia, PhishIntention, PhishLLM, KnowPhish, PhishAgent, PiMRef) are the strongest semantic idea: they check whether a claim is true, not how text looks. Prompted LLMs (ChatSpamDetector, ChatPhishDetector) report 98 to 99.7% without training, on small balanced sets at real cost per message. Fine-tuned transformers report 97 to 99.9% on clean data, but those numbers say little about real use.

#### Phishing email content

- **[PR, FT, cit 100] Takashi Koide, Naoki Fukushi, Hiroki Nakano, Daiki Chiba (2024). "ChatSpamDetector: Leveraging Large Language Models for Effective Phishing Email Detection." SecureComm 2024.** [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.18093). A journal version by Koide, Nakano and Chiba appeared in [IEEE Access 2026](https://doi.org/10.1109/ACCESS.2026.3704566) with the same 99.70% figure. Method: turns headers and body into a prompt for GPT-4; no training; returns a rationale. Data: 2,010 emails; 1,010 phishing from Aug 2022 to Oct 2023 plus 1,000 legitimate from CSDMC. Result: accuracy 99.70% with GPT-4, 96.92% with GPT-3.5, best baseline 86.22%. Limit: emails without URLs were excluded, so payload-free BEC is not covered. GPT-4 cost $265.80 for the 2,010 emails, about $0.13 per email. Limit (mine): 50/50 balance is far from real base rates.
- **[PR, Abs, cit 160] Yong Fang, Cheng Zhang, Cheng Huang, Liang Liu, Yue Yang (2019). "Phishing Email Detection Using Improved RCNN Model With Multilevel Vectors and Attention Mechanism" (THEMIS). #### Business email compromise

- **[PR, Abs, cit 75] Asaf Cidon, Lior Gavish, Itay Bleier, Nadia Korshun, Marco Schweighauser, Alexey Tsitkin (2019). "High Precision Detection of Business Email Compromise." Limit (mine): private data.
- **[Pre, FT, cit 6] Jan Brabec, Filip Šrajer, Radek Starosta, Tomáš Sixta, Marc Dupont, Miloš Lenoch, Jiří Menšík, Florian Becker, Jakub Boros, Tomáš Pop, Pavel Novák (2023). - **[PR?, Abs, cit 1] Yaw Osei Adjei, Frederick Ayivor (2025). ## Which surveys and benchmarks exist?

### Takeaway

Surveys are plentiful. The most useful recent ones are the Chen 2025 SoK (it also benchmarks detectors), Sivaneswaran 2026 (a PRISMA review of LLMs and phishing), Pritom 2026 (smishing, including dataset checks), Tian 2025 (URLs) and Kritika 2026 (quishing). Benchmarks are newer and weaker, and most test LLMs as victims or use small sets. PhreshPhish is the best evaluation design found.

## How do transformer and LLM methods compare with classical machine learning and with rule-based filters?

### Takeaway

On clean English data, transformers lead classical ML by 1 to 3 points, and prompted LLMs beat fixed baselines by a lot on small tests. Rule-based and commercial filters are weakest against LLM-rephrased text. But the gap is not universal. Classical models beat a transformer on Bangla-English fraud and on small synthetic conversation data; small quantised LLMs lose to Naive Bayes on accuracy; and simple rules reached 99.22% on Pakistani lottery fraud. Fine-tuning beats prompting when labels exist. Prompting generalises better to unseen scams, but with weak precision.

- Fine-tuning beat prompting on URLs, F1 97.29% against 92.74% ([Trad and Chehab](https://doi.org/10.3390/make6010018)).

- LLMs generalise to unseen scams better than a fine-tuned BERT, but scam precision capped at 69% ([Topcuoglu et al.](https://doi.org/10.1109/COMPSAC69091.2026.00090)).

### Inferences

- A cascade is better supported than a single model. Three independent results show the gain: SocketAI pre-screening cut files by 77.9%; Scam Shield routes most cases away from the LLM; and Trad and Chehab's [two-tier agents](https://doi.org/10.1109/FLLM63129.2024.10852444) cut API cost. Suggested STL order: rules and reputation, then a compact model, then an LLM only for unclear cases.
- Keep a classical baseline in every STL experiment. In short, low-resource, code-mixed text it may win, and a win over it is what makes a transformer claim believable.
- Do not use Gmail, Outlook or commercial app results as stable facts. Vendors change filters often, so those tests are snapshots.

### Gaps

- Few studies test rule-based filters, classical ML, fine-tuned transformers and LLMs on the same data, with temporal splits, at low base rate. I found none across all six threats.
- A Teesside blog reportedly gives per-provider numbers for Opara et al. (Yahoo blocked 90%; Gmail let 86% through; Outlook 96%), and another listing says GPT-4 rather than GPT-4o and 47 features rather than 60. I saw this only in search snippets and could not open the blog, so treat those numbers as unverified.

## How robust are these methods to LLM-written phishing and to adversarial rewording?

### Takeaway

Not robust by default. LLM-written mail now matches human experts in click rate. Rephrasing cuts detection in commercial filters and classical models by about 5 to 10 points. Robustness depends strongly on which generator wrote the text, so one test proves little. Under adversarial rewording, transformers and TF-IDF can fail equally, though transformers resist character tricks far better. LLM detectors add a new weakness, prompt injection, with attack success from 39.9% to 84.7% before defenses. The best answers so far are adversarial retraining, diverse ensembles, and fact-based checks.

### Inferences

- Do not build STL around "this text is AI-written". Legitimate users also write with LLMs, and the SoK shows detector results swing by generator. Detect intent and false claims.
- Report robustness as a table: per generator, per attack family, at fixed FPR. One adversarial accuracy number hides too much.
- Treat all content an LLM reads as hostile input, and validate output against a fixed schema. Koide's result suggests this matters more than the choice of model.
- STL's two-layer design helps here. Text can be reworded cheaply, while reused hosting, domain age and certificate timing are harder to change.

### Gaps

- I did not find a large peer-reviewed study of LLM-written smishing against deployed filters, or any test of LLM-written Roman Urdu or Urdu fraud.
- Adversarial sets are small: 275 in Ahmed 2026, 189 in Hasan 2025, about 71 malicious test messages in Chiuseni 2026.
- [arXiv 2507.02699](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.02699) (Your Mailbox Is Mine, NDSS 2027 per its comment) and [arXiv 2609.30657](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.30657) (email agent prompt injection, ICTAI 2026 per its comment) appeared in listings only; I did not read them.

## What exists for QR phishing, for SMS scams in South Asian languages, and for on-device inference?

### Takeaway

QR work is young, mostly synthetic, and I found no LLM-based detector for it. Image-only results range from 83% accuracy and 0.91 AUC on open, well-described setups up to 99% on less transparent ones, and decoded-URL analysis stays the practical route. South Asian coverage is the biggest gap: Bangla has real work, Hindi is minimal, and Roman Urdu has only classical models on small hand-built sets, with no transformer smishing paper found. On-device is the healthiest area, with shipped products and small models (3.46 MB VAE, 4.5M-parameter BiLSTM, MobileBERT), but no support for Urdu or Roman Urdu was found in any of them.

### Inferences

- For quishing, STL should decode the QR and reuse its URL and page pipeline. Image-only analysis mainly adds value when decoding is unsafe. The 91% image AUC may reflect the URL characters encoded in the pixels, not any visual sign of phishing; that is my reading.
- For Roman Urdu, the strongest project niche is clear: no transformer smishing paper, no public fraud dataset per Ayaz et al., and strong evidence that spelling variance is the core problem. A plausible plan is a char-aware model (Hosseinpour, RETVec style), RUBERT or a multilingual encoder, a classical baseline, and real messages for testing. Synthetic data can help training, but test on real messages only.
- Measure false positives per language first. LLMPEA suggests that a model tuned on English may flag normal Urdu or Hindi at several times its English rate.
- A realistic STL split: compact model on device or gateway for every message; heavier analysis only for flagged ones. Google's Chrome design is a working example, and PhishLang shows the same idea for webpages.

### Gaps

- I found no peer-reviewed transformer or LLM smishing study on Urdu script or Roman Urdu; I also found none on Hindi, Tamil, Telugu or Sinhala specifically. Absence in my searches does not prove none exist.
- Open-source Pakistani projects exist ([Muhafiz-AI](https://github.com/saifkhandev/Muhafiz-AI), [roman-urdu-scam-detector](https://github.com/Sawera-Ali/roman-urdu-scam-detector)). Their claims, such as 99.6% adversarial accuracy, are self-reported and not research evidence.
- [Karandaaz](https://www.karandaaz.com.pk/research/publications/sms-fraud-detection-prevention-pakistan) has a Pakistan SMS fraud publication that surfaced in search; I did not open it. Pritom et al. cite [on-device LLM explanation work](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.11429) by Wang et al. and by Sanjari et al.; I did not trace these.
- I found no LLM-based quishing detector, and no study on real QR codes collected from the wild at scale.
- Google's RETVec post returned no body text, so production Gmail numbers are not verified here.
- I did not verify any on-device phone benchmark (battery, memory, latency on a mid-range Android). Most numbers above come from GPU or desktop setups.

## Which public datasets are used, and what are their known weaknesses?

### Takeaway

The field rests on old, English, and often near-duplicate corpora. Random splits cause leakage, 50/50 balance hides the base-rate problem, and URLs go dead so page features cannot be rebuilt. LLM-era data is mostly self-generated. Results from different papers are rarely comparable, since even the same corpus appears at different sizes.

#### QR and packages

- QR datasets are nearly all generated from URL lists, so they carry URL-list flaws and look unlike real posters or stickers ([Trad and Chehab](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.03451); [Akram et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.17175); [Nejati et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.09015)). The review says private lab datasets block comparison ([Kritika](https://doi.org/10.1080/23742917.2026.2696261)).
- Package detectors were tested on disparate datasets until the unified 13,708-package benchmark, and ML ages as malware gets simpler ([Guo et al.](https://doi.org/10.1145/3832783.3837427)).

### Inferences

- Base rate matters more than most papers admit. Using the PhreshPhish precision formula: at recall 0.9 and base rate 0.05%, an FPR of 0.1% gives precision near 31%, and an FPR of 1% gives near 4%. This is my calculation. A "99% accurate" filter can still flag mostly innocent messages.
- For STL, split data by time and by campaign or kit, never at random. Check near-duplicates first, since Ayaz et al. hit this directly with Roman Urdu fraud messages.
- Plan your own Roman Urdu collection early. This needs consent and privacy handling, since real SMS holds personal data. Pervaiz et al. give a tested method: an opt-in app, a forwarding number and WhatsApp submissions.
- Page and link features must be captured when a message arrives. The 1,645 of 7,759 live-URL figure shows they cannot be rebuilt later.
