# STL's Parts Exist, Its Join Is Untested

**No public system does what STL plans to do.** Searches on 5 November 2026 found no project, product or paper that reads one event by meaning at both the network layer and the application layer, joins the two findings into one verdict, signs that verdict so others can check it, and offers both a user app and a server dashboard. Darktrace comes closest among products, AiSOC and Slips among open-source projects, and Sentinel-AI among student-style projects, yet each misses at least two of those parts. Every single part exists somewhere, so **what is new in STL is the join, not the detectors**, and that join is unproven: in the best controlled study found, a second data source at a 1% false alarm rate helped only two of seven models and hurt the other five, and that study joined radio and network data, not network and application data ([Fard et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.22450)). **The honest research gap is therefore a measurement**: does a second layer improve detection at a low false alarm rate, and can one layer catch the other when an attacker fools it? STL cannot measure this yet, because no public dataset was found that links one event across both layers. "By meaning, not by fixed rules" is also too strong, because rules still do much of the work: a simple five-signal rule flagged 891 of 898 Pakistani fraud messages ([Pervaiz et al.](https://doi.org/10.1145/3314344.3332500)), and fixed non-AI checks stopped 78% of blocked attacks in a 2026 preprint on securing agent systems ([Mitra et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.07657)). The team should aim first at a short paper or poster at SVCC 2027 or a similar venue, then extend it into a journal such as IEEE Access. "Not found" in this report means absent from these searches, not proven absent, and this field changes monthly.

The rows show a clear split. Enterprise tools see networks but not a person's SMS inbox, and consumer tools see messages but not traffic flows. STL sits across that split, which is its clearest selling point. The method differs too. Darktrace's AI learns each organisation's normal behaviour rather than relying on signatures ([Darktrace](https://www.darktrace.com/platform)), while STL asks what the content means. The nearest real case of both kinds of evidence on one event is Microsoft's Phishing Triage Agent. A large language model (LLM) judges the intent of an email and can hunt across other data, but it runs only after a user reports the email, and it needs Security Copilot capacity plus Defender for Office 365 Plan 2 ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/defender-xdr/phishing-triage-agent)).

Student projects look close but are not. Sentinel-AI feeds separate URL, flow, malware and YARA checks into one 0-100 score, but that score combines one result's confidence, severity and asset value, not two layers on one event. QuantShield has separate routes for email, SMS, URL and network checks, and a keyword search of its `app.py` found no fusion code. A marker can tell several models behind one dashboard from real fusion, so **STL's demo must show one event where neither layer alone gives the right verdict**.

Among papers, four come close in different ways, and none is STL. By wording, the closest is "Semantic Multi-Agent Intrusion Detection for IoT", but its author has asked to withdraw it because the manuscript "does not sufficiently substantiate all conclusions" ([Jamshidi](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.10323)), so never cite it as evidence. By shape, the closest is MALCDF, where four LLM agents exchange encrypted messages, yet all its numbers come from one 50-record stream ([Bhardwaj et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.14846)). By vocabulary, it is AScope, which links agent-level meaning to kernel events but protects agent systems and covers application plus operating system, not network traffic ([Cai et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.04469)). By evidence mix, it is PhishLumos, whose LLM agents trace shared hosting, certificates and domain registration ([Chiba et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.21772)).

The name itself is free in network security but crowded nearby. A GitHub search for the exact phrase returned three repositories, none of them this idea: [AiNIR](https://github.com/hamlet-lab/AiNIR) guards AI-agent actions, [SemanticTrustLayer](https://github.com/pvchaitu/SemanticTrustLayer) detects LLM jailbreaks and also uses the short form STL, and [NexusBI](https://github.com/HeyderHesenov/NexusBI) is a business analytics tool. Salesforce's [Einstein Trust Layer](https://trailhead.salesforce.com/content/learn/modules/the-einstein-trust-layer/meet-the-einstein-trust-layer) protects LLM use, and an [arXiv title search](https://export.arxiv.org/api/query?search_query=ti:%22trust%20layer%22) for "trust layer" returned six 2026 papers, five on AI agents and one on plasma physics. No trademark check was done, and a clearer paper title would reduce confusion.

Two words need a definition in the first paragraph of any STL paper. "Semantic" is used for 6G semantic communication, which protects meaning during transmission ([Meng et al.](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jnca.2025.104181)), for semantic routing, which moves traffic by content or intent ([IETF draft](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/draft-cui-semantic-routing-ai-agent-communication/)), and for reasoning about intent, which is what STL does. Citing the first two as direct prior art is an error a reviewer will catch. A web firewall can even call its SQL and XSS engines semantic ([uusec-waf](https://github.com/Safe3/uusec-waf)). "Cross-layer" is just as loose: it can mean OSI layers, radio versus flows, application versus operating system, or neural-network layers.

**STL should not claim that nobody uses AI across layers**, because Darktrace and Microsoft correlate across domains. The fair claim, already written in the project's architecture document, is an open design that checks one event by meaning at both layers and gives a verdict others can verify. These searches cover public pages only. Private code, GitLab, Gitee and closed products could hold similar work, and vendors do not publish enough to show whether their AI reads one event at both layers or only joins alerts afterwards. CrowdStrike, Palo Alto, Cisco and SentinelOne were not checked.

## Related papers fall into five groups, and top scores rarely survive real data

The tables below are a reading list, not the full field. The five research notes behind this report hold hundreds of entries, in `research_notes/AI semantic trust layer research/`. **Status** tells you how far to trust a paper. *Reviewed* means a journal, conference or workshop accepted it and the record was confirmed. *Claimed* means the authors state acceptance but no published record was confirmed, so treat it as a preprint until you find the record. *Preprint* means no peer review was found. All numbers are the authors' own, and none was reproduced for this report.

Some terms repeat. *Recall* is the share of bad items caught. *Precision* is the share of flagged items that are really bad. *False alarm rate* is the share of harmless items wrongly flagged. *F1* and *macro-F1* combine precision and recall, and macro-F1 treats every class equally. *AUC* runs from 0.5 for guessing to 1 for perfect ranking. *Zero-shot* means the model gets no examples, and *few-shot* means a few in the prompt.

The idea that matters most is the **base rate**, meaning how rare bad items are. If 1 flow in 10,000 is malicious and a detector catches all of them, a 1% false alarm rate still gives precision of about 1%, and reaching 50% precision needs a false alarm rate near 0.01%. That arithmetic explains why a "99% accurate" detector can flag mostly innocent items, and why a real security centre saw only 0.01% of 115 million alerts tied to true attacks ([Yang et al.](https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity24/presentation/yang-limin)).

### Fraud papers: checking a claim beats judging the style

The best evidence comes from production systems that report results at very low false alarm rates, such as BEC-Guard and URLTran below. Those numbers mean more than the 99% scores common on clean public data. The idea that best fits "by meaning" is **reference-based detection**. Instead of asking whether text looks like phishing, it asks whether a claim is true, for example whether a page showing a bank's logo really sits on that bank's domain. Prompted LLMs look strong on small balanced tests and weak elsewhere, as the ChatSpamDetector and Topcuoglu rows show. Attackers have LLMs too. Fully automated spear phishing matched human experts at 54% click-through in a 101-person study ([Heiding et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.00586)), and LLM rephrasing cut Proofpoint's recall from 98.95% to 93.16% ([Afane et al.](https://doi.org/10.1109/BigData62323.2024.10825018)).

Two gaps matter for this project. No peer-reviewed transformer or LLM smishing study on Urdu or Roman Urdu was found, even though most Pakistani SMS fraud is written in Roman Urdu. Direct work on trojanised installers is also thin, so the closest evidence comes from malicious packages and from LLMs reading sandbox reports ([Saul et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.00297)).

### Abuse papers: veiled threats, quoted slurs and formal email are the weak spots

**No method survives contact with real data.** On a true random day of tweets, no model reached both precision and recall above 50% in any context, and the authors say public models are unfit for automatic moderation without human oversight ([Tonneau et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.15462)). Real hate was only 0.7% of tweets there on average, so datasets with over 50% hateful items are artificial.

The project's Rector example has an easy form and two hard forms. A slur aimed at the reader is the easy form. The hard forms are a threat with no bad word, and an email that contains a slur because it quotes or reports one. Models treat any mention of a slur as abuse, so they punish people who report abuse and people who reclaim a word. STL needs an explicit use-or-mention step, and `stl/reviewer.py` already tells the language model that quoting or reporting is not an offence. Whether that works on Urdu is unknown. For veiled threats, a realistic target is macro-F1 near 0.7 to 0.8 on a clean balanced test, with much lower precision at real inbox base rates.

Email is almost unstudied. One peer-reviewed enterprise corpus exists, and no public corpus of threats sent to officials or universities was found. Nothing was found for implicit hate, veiled threats or quoted abuse in Urdu or Roman Urdu. Google's Perspective API, the usual comparison tool, stops on 31 December 2026 ([Perspective](https://perspectiveapi.com/)), so do not build on it.

### Network papers: metadata carries the signal and simple models often win

Encryption hides content, so STL's Network Agent must judge from sizes, directions, timing and connection patterns. The evidence supports that split: a 2025 study found classifiers rely on payload length, not on patterns in the ciphertext ([Wickramasinghe et al.](https://doi.org/10.1109/SP61157.2025.00165)). "Meaning" at this layer can only mean behaviour, and Exosphere is the best example, since it learns the semantics of packet length patterns with no contents at all.

**The biggest warning is an independent re-test.** Models that claimed F1 above 95% fell to between 0.5 and 24.0 macro-F1 on a 120-website TLS task, while LightGBM on header fields reached 82.4 with IP addresses and 40.6 without them ([Zhao et al.](https://doi.org/10.1145/3718958.3750498)). That test froze the encoders and worked on single packets from older data, so it is harsh on models built for whole flows, but the lesson stands: run a simple baseline and remove shortcut fields. LLMs make poor detectors here. Zero-shot GPT-4 on raw flow records scored about 50% macro precision and recall, and Llama 3 took around 7,000 times longer per sample than Random Forest. They work better as explainers fed by a separate detector. Unseen attacks are the true test, so STL should expect those scores to fall far below its seen-attack scores and treat that as normal, not as failure.

### Cross-layer and multi-agent papers: fusion is unproven and LLM agents can be fooled

**Direct evidence on fusion, meaning the joining of data sources, is thin and mixed.** Fard et al. is the model to copy: seven architectures, ten seeds, test runs kept apart from training runs, and results at 1% false alarms. Gains appeared only where both single sources were weak, with detection below 0.75. Joining application logs with lower-level logs has strong support, but for forensics on a host after an attack, not live detection. **No study was found that measures the gain from joining network-layer and application-layer meaning-based agents.**

Multi-agent LLM work is crowded, so "several agents beat one" is now a baseline, not a finding. The most useful recent result is negative: in AIDA's benchmark every baseline triage method missed at least 40.4% of attack alerts until an independent challenger and judge were added. Any LLM that reads attacker-written text can also be steered by instructions hidden in that text, which is called prompt injection, as the LogInject row shows.

The evidence on rules versus meaning points two ways, and both are right. Hand-written rules moved semantic categories by 0 points in AgentTrust, so rules cannot see intent. Yet fixed non-AI checks stopped most blocked attacks in Mitra et al., a study of agent security rather than incoming data, and practitioners want ML to work with rules, not replace them ([Mink et al.](https://doi.org/10.1109/SP46215.2023.10179321)). **The supported design is rules and fast models first, with meaning-based judgement for what is left.**

### Trust papers: a signature proves who spoke, not that the speaker was right

A signature shows who said something and that nobody changed it. It does not show the statement is true. Threat sharing fails mostly on quality, not authenticity, and one preprint tested this directly: signature-only protocols accepted most lies from a compromised agent. So STL's verdict needs evidence attached, plus a way to score each peer's past accuracy.

The building blocks are mature standards: [COSE](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9052.html) for the signed envelope, [SCITT](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9943.html) for the public log pattern, [STIX](https://docs.oasis-open.org/cti/stix/v2.1/os/stix-v2.1-os.html) for vocabulary and [TAXII](https://docs.oasis-open.org/cti/taxii/v2.1/os/taxii-v2.1-os.html) for transport. A keyword scan of the STIX text found no signing terms, so a verdict appears to need an outer envelope. **No paper was found that defines a signed detection verdict and tests it between distrusting operators.** Federated learning is mature, so it should stay a side feature. Claims about 6G need a radio-network test, which STL's plan leaves out, so 6G belongs in future work.

**The strongest idea is one layer checking the other.** An email body or log field is text the attacker writes. A flow's timing, volume and peer are harder to fake, because attackers do not control all network features equally ([Li 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.07739)). So if an application agent is fooled into calling a message safe, a network agent working from facts the attacker controls less can still disagree. Disagreement then becomes a deception detector. This is an inference that joins separate papers, not a published result. It fails if an attacker shapes both layers, for example through a compromised host that generates normal-looking traffic.

**STL's own code settles one open question and raises two more.** The research notes saw only `pyproject.toml` and one data script, found no LLM client in the dependencies, and guessed that STL's agents were compact models. They warned that a plain HTTP call would not show up in that check, and that is what happened. `stl/reviewer.py` calls a local language model (`qwen3.5:9b` through Ollama) for unclear items, so STL is a hybrid: fast models first, language model second. This is the staged design the literature supports, and it means both attack families apply: prompt injection against the language model, and evasion, which changes an input so a model misses it, against the compact models.

The first new issue is the join. `stl/coordinator.py` treats the agents as two independent witnesses, with joined risk = 1 − (1 − application risk) × (1 − network risk). This rule can only raise risk, so the joined system inherits false alarms from both agents, and the independence assumption is untested. Fard et al. is the reason to measure this at a fixed false alarm rate, not assume it. The second issue is an attack path in `stl/agents.py`. When the language model answers with no labels, the application risk drops, so a message that injects that answer lowers the application risk while the network risk stays put. That is exactly the case the core idea needs, and `needs_review` already sends content that looks clear to the language model when the network agent is worried. Signing today detects tampering, not lying: `stl/signing.py` gives each agent an Ed25519 key, but all the keys sit as plain files on one machine. The architecture document also records that the models are not trained and no test results exist, so none of these claims can be made yet.

**The dataset problem blocks the main claim.** STL's sources each cover one layer, from unrelated times and networks. A CICIDS2017 flow and a Nazario email share no session, host or attack. `training/evaluate_system.py` says this openly and pairs held-out content with held-out traffic histories in four groups, and the architecture document states the limit. There is a further catch, which is a reading of that script and not a published finding. Harmful content is paired with normal traffic and bad traffic with fine content, so each agent alone misses a whole group by construction, and joined recall must beat either agent whatever a second layer is really worth. **Keep experiment E7 as a system test, but do not report it as fusion gain.** Three routes to real evidence exist, cheapest first. One is to extract application content from inside one capture, such as HTTP payloads in the original CICIDS2017 traces, though whether those files allow this was not checked. Another is a small testbed that creates both layers together, and STL's SMTP gateway and HTTP proxy already see both layers of one session. Those results would be lab data and should be reported as such. The last is to accept that the agents work on separate tasks and drop the fusion-gain claim. Link feeds carry a related risk: phishing feeds set against Tranco can teach a model "popular domain" instead of "safe domain", and feeds change daily, so split by time ([Arp et al.](https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity22/presentation/arp)).

**A paper that fits three undergraduates is a measurement study, not a system description.** The first question asks how much each layer detects alone and how much both detect together, at a fixed 1% false alarm rate with test runs kept apart from training runs, which needs paired data. The second asks how often attacks succeed against one layer compared with both, using prompt injection in the style of LogInject on the language model and constrained evasion in the style of Li 2026 on the compact models. The third asks how far a false verdict spreads when one peer lies, comparing no signature, signature only, and signature plus evidence, with latency and message size. Each question should also compare rules alone, model alone, and rules then model, because STL's design has no rule stage and reviewers will ask. Report per-class results, unseen attack families, realistic base rates and classical baselines such as TF-IDF and LightGBM, and publish the code and raw outputs.

**If paired data proves too hard, three narrower papers are easier to finish and less crowded.** One is Roman Urdu fraud or smishing detection, which needs a new labelled set because the project's Roman Urdu data is hate speech. Pervaiz et al. give a tested collection method: an opt-in app, a forwarding number and WhatsApp submissions, with consent and privacy handling. Another is a small Urdu and Roman Urdu test set for veiled threats and quoted abuse, growing from STL's 57 hand-written messages, which the architecture document rightly says are not yet a benchmark. The third is signed verdicts alone, tested against a lying peer.

Three risks remain. The field moves monthly: PCM appeared on 29 October 2026 and StateFence on 2 November 2026, so re-run these searches a week before submission. Absence from searches is not proof, so write "to the best of our knowledge" and list the search terms. IEEE Xplore and the ACM Digital Library were not searched directly, and the comparison rests mostly on abstracts, so a reviewer may find features the abstracts leave out.

## Conclusion

This review changes what STL should try to prove. The project began as a claim that a new kind of system works. The evidence says the more valuable result is knowing when a second layer helps and when it only adds false alarms, and that answer is publishable either way, because no study was found that measures it for network plus application meaning. A negative result at 1% false alarms would still be new, so a failed fusion experiment is not a failed project.

The best idea here came from reading the literatures together, not from any one paper: treat the network layer less as a second detector and more as a lie detector for the first. Text can be rewritten cheaply and LLMs can be talked into things, while timing and volume are harder to fake. If that holds, STL's real contribution is robustness, not coverage, and the team has two assets large labs lack: a gateway where one real session produces both layers, and the ability to read and label Urdu and Roman Urdu. Those two assets, more than the agents or the signatures, are what a reviewer has not seen before.
